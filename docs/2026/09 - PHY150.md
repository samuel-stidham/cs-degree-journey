**Course Description:**
In this algebra-based physics course, students will explore the major fundamental topics in physics as they relate to mechanics, such as motion and forces, gravity and projectiles, and energy and work. Through their exploration of these topics and embedded lab work, students will learn to describe the motion of objects in both one and two dimensions, and to solve problems through the application of Newton’s Laws of Motion. Additionally, they will also apply the principles of the conservation of energy and momentum to analyze the behavior of interacting objects.

---

## Key Topics & Concepts

This algebra-based mechanics course builds a foundation in classical physics through paired theory and embedded lab work:

* **Kinematics:** Describing motion in one and two dimensions using distance, displacement, speed, velocity, and acceleration.
* **Newton's Laws of Motion:** Applying the three laws (inertia, $F = ma$, action-reaction) to analyze forces acting on interacting objects.
* **Gravity & Projectile Motion:** Modeling free-fall and projectile trajectories by decomposing motion into independent horizontal and vertical components.
* **Work & Energy:** Calculating kinetic and potential energy, and applying the work-energy theorem to mechanical systems.
* **Conservation Laws:** Applying conservation of energy and conservation of momentum to predict outcomes of interactions such as collisions.
* **Foundational Competencies:**
  * **PHY-20656:** Describe the motion of an object in one and two dimensions.
  * **PHY-20657:** Apply Newton's laws of motion to solve problems.
  * **PHY-20658:** Apply principles of conservation of energy and momentum to analyze the behavior of interacting objects.

## Major Projects & Assignments

All three projects were framed as deliverables for a simulated client, "A&L Engineering." Each project required a diagram, a step-by-step kinematics/dynamics analysis, and a written explanation of the physical reasoning behind every equation used.

1. **Project One: Supply Drop Plan (Objects in Motion)**
    * **Focus:** Two-dimensional projectile motion for an aerial supply drop, analyzed by treating horizontal and vertical motion as independent components.
    * **Application:** Calculated fall time, horizontal displacement, and resultant impact velocity for a payload released from a plane at 250 mph and 2,650 m. Then combined the velocity components via the Pythagorean theorem.
    * **Scenario Adaptations:** Re-ran the analysis under two changed conditions. The first was a real-world headwind sourced from a live National Weather Service report. It reduced horizontal velocity and shortened the drop distance. The second was a catapult launch at an angle. This scenario required resolving the launch velocity into components with sine/cosine before determining whether the trajectory cleared a target height.

2. **Project Two: Case Material Evaluation Report (Newton's Laws)**
    * **Focus:** Applying all three of Newton's laws to a phone-drop test comparing protective case materials.
    * **Application:** Modeled a 2.0 m drop in two phases across four cases: no case, silicone, hard plastic, and rubber. The free-fall phase used the time-independent kinematic equation to find impact velocity. The impact phase used collision time to find deceleration, then Newton's second law, $F = ma$, to find impact force.
    * **Conclusion:** Identified the rubber case as offering the best protection, since its longer collision time produced the lowest impact force (20.9 N). It produced that low force despite adding the most mass. Then weighed that result against real-world trade-offs like bulk, grip, and cost.

3. **Project Three: Roller Coaster Design Report (Energy and Momentum)**
    * **Focus:** Tracking the interplay of potential energy, kinetic energy, and momentum across a roller coaster track. The analysis then extended through a collision and a friction-based stop.
    * **Application:** Calculated PE and KE at five track points (launch, two valleys, two peaks). The calculations confirmed that total mechanical energy stays constant when friction is ignored. Then modeled a perfectly inelastic collision between two carts using conservation of momentum. The momentum analysis gave the post-collision velocity and the kinetic energy lost to heat, sound, and deformation.
    * **Extension:** Applied the work-energy theorem to a final friction-based stop. Calculated the frictional force required to bring the combined cart to rest over a fixed distance. Tied each energy "loss" back to conservation of energy by accounting for where the energy actually went.

4. **Lab: Kinematics**
    * **Focus:** Building graphical and physical intuition for position, velocity, and acceleration across five short activities.
    * **Application:** Interpreted a position-vs-time graph of a train that sped up, stopped, and reversed direction. This distinguished total distance traveled from net displacement and identified graph slope as instantaneous/average velocity. Used an inclined-ramp experiment with steel and acrylic spheres to confirm two results. Displacement is proportional to time squared under constant acceleration (a linear distance-vs-$t^2$ plot). An object's mass has no effect on its acceleration due to gravity.
    * **Observation-Based Confirmation:** Used a ramp with evenly and unevenly spaced rubber-band markers to audibly confirm constant acceleration. Equally spaced markers produced sounds that sped up over time. Markers spaced according to the $d = \frac{1}{2}at^2$ relationship produced a steady, evenly timed beat.

5. **Lab: Newton's Second Law**
    * **Focus:** Verifying the force-acceleration relationship $F = ma$ through graphical analysis of a cart accelerated by a hanging mass. Washers transfer from the cart to the hanging paperclip, so the accelerating force grows while total system mass stays constant.
    * **Application:** Ran five configurations (three to seven suspended washers) with three timed trials each. Derived the acceleration formula $a = 2d/t^2$ from the kinematics equation $d = \frac{1}{2}at^2$, valid because the cart starts from rest. Plotted force against acceleration, where Newton's Second Law predicts the slope equals the system mass. The slope gave 0.1148 kg against the scale-measured 0.1180 kg, a 2.75% difference.
    * **Error Analysis:** Attributed the gap to stopwatch reaction time, pulley and wheel friction, parallax, and the pocket scale's limited precision. Noted that friction consumes driving force and lowers measured acceleration. Because the slope represents mass, that steepens the line and biases the experimental mass upward. The measured slope mass came out low instead, so other error sources outweighed friction here.
    * **Concept Questions:** Explained that doubling net force doubles acceleration at constant mass. Also analyzed a vehicle at constant highway velocity. Zero acceleration means the net force is zero, not that no forces act. The driving force exactly balances friction and air resistance, tying the Second Law back to the First.

6. **Lab: Momentum**
    * **Focus:** Verifying the law of conservation of momentum across three cart-collision trials with varying mass distributions. The trials used equal masses, added mass on Cart A, and added mass on Cart B.
    * **Application:** Calculated system momentum before and after each collision from cart velocities measured by hand-timed trials. Percent differences between the theoretical and measured values ranged from 15.3% to 31.1%.
    * **Error Analysis:** Attributed the discrepancies to friction and human reaction-time error in the sub-second timing measurements rather than a failure of the conservation law. In one trial, the lighter cart showed a reversed (negative) post-collision velocity. Explained how that reversal was handled by preserving the vector sign of momentum throughout the calculation.

## Discussion Work & Academic Themes

1. **Newton's Third Law: Squid Jet Propulsion**
    * **Focus:** Used squid locomotion as a real-world action-reaction pair to illustrate Newton's Third Law.
    * **Analysis:** Explained how a squid draws water into its mantle cavity and expels it backward through the hyponome. The hyponome is a muscular funnel acting as a nozzle. The squid's backward push on the expelled water is the action force. The water's equal and opposite reaction force pushes the squid forward. Emphasized the often-missed nuance that the action and reaction forces act on two different bodies (squid and water), not the same object. That is the mechanism that lets the squid accelerate.

2. **Conservation of Energy: From Power Plant to Pixel, and a Battery's Inner Life**
    * **Original Post Focus:** Traced the full energy transformation chain behind a gaming PC. The chain starts with chemical energy in coal/natural gas burned at local power plants. It continues through grid transmission and AC-to-DC conversion in the power supply, ending with consumption by the CPU/GPU and peripherals. Nearly all of that energy ends as waste heat. Concluded that energy is always conserved, but becomes progressively less *useful* as it disperses, distinguishing conservation from usefulness.
    * **Instructor Follow-Up:** Asked for a diagram of an AA alkaline battery's internal components and an explanation of why batteries degrade over time. Also asked whether a battery's energy should be considered "conserved" given that it eventually dies.
    * **My Response (Extended the Analysis Further):**
        * **Original Diagram:** After finding that reference battery diagrams were copyrighted, built an original Excalidraw cross-section of an AA alkaline cell. The diagram labeled the steel can, cathode (manganese dioxide), separator, anode (zinc paste), KOH electrolyte, brass collector pin, and terminals. Cited DoITPoMS for component identification.
        * **Battery Degradation:** Explained that a cell "dies" because its zinc and manganese dioxide reactants are consumed during discharge. That consumption raises internal resistance until the cell can no longer sustain voltage. This is a depletion of usable chemical reactants, not a loss of energy.
        * **Conservation Argument:** Argued the battery's total energy remains conserved under the First Law of Thermodynamics. Chemical potential energy converts to electrical energy and then to thermal energy without ever disappearing. The energy only becomes less useful as it disperses (Urone & Hinrichs, 2022, Section 15.1).
        * **Pushed the Reasoning Further:** Proactively raised and answered a follow-on question: whether cooled, dispersed heat is still conserved. Concluded yes, since cooling is a transfer to the surroundings, not a destruction of energy. Tied the declining *usefulness* of dispersed heat to entropy and the Second Law of Thermodynamics (Urone & Hinrichs, 2022, Section 15.6).
    * **Takeaway:** The response moved beyond the original post's qualitative energy-tracing exercise into a sourced, two-law argument. That argument cleanly separated the conservation of energy from its usefulness. It applied the same reasoning from the PC example to a real chemical system. It then extended that reasoning one step further than the instructor's question asked.

## Personal Reflection & Takeaways

My view of physics shifted significantly over the course of this term. I went in expecting the math to be a barrier. I carried old assumptions that formulas and dense terminology like "entropy" put the subject out of reach. That assumption didn't hold up. Once I slowed down and worked through the steps, the math was approachable. Once I understood what a term like entropy actually described (a measure of a system's disorder), the intimidation around it faded. That pattern repeated for nearly every concept in the course.

The course also changed how much physics I notice in everyday situations. Midway through the term, I flew to a work conference in Lincoln, Nebraska. On that single flight, I caught myself tracing all three of Newton's Laws. I saw the thrust needed to overcome the plane's inertia at rest. I saw the second law governing how much force is needed to accelerate or decelerate that much mass. The third law showed up twice. It appeared once in the engines, where exhaust expelled backward pushes the aircraft forward. It appeared again in the landing gear's action-reaction pair on touchdown. Before this course, none of that would have crossed my mind.

The two concepts that stuck with me most were Newton's Laws and thermodynamics. In Newton's Laws, it was the elegant simplicity of $F = ma$ and the action-reaction pairing from my squid propulsion post. In thermodynamics, it was the idea that energy is never created or destroyed, only converted. Each conversion increases disorder (entropy) and makes the energy less useful over time. Working through both ideas left me with a new interest I didn't expect going in. I want to make physics more approachable for people scared off by math or vocabulary before they reach the underlying concept. That is the spirit of communicators like Carl Sagan and Neil deGrasse Tyson.
